# Model selection for play — weighing intelligence, latency, throughput, and price per machine role

Researched 2026-09-01 for the [design brief](../design-brief.md). Question: how should model **intelligence, time-to-first-token, tokens/s, and price** be weighed for each of the machine's three roles — the **Author** (builds Pages before play), the **Meter** (grades meaning-closeness live, at the reveal, on the couch), and the **Scribe** (writes the Chronicle after play) — when fun is the only v1 objective and cost is explicitly no object? Jacob is comparing models on [artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models](https://artificialanalysis.ai/leaderboards/models) and wants to read it correctly.

Method: every claim traces to a primary source — Artificial Analysis's own methodology pages (version and date noted), the original HCI papers, arXiv/ACL papers, and vendor docs — all fetched 2026-09-01. Secondary sources are labeled. No commercial model is named or recommended; the output is a *profile* per role. Provider pricing tables are handled elsewhere. Prior-art facts already cited in [llm-in-word-games.md](llm-in-word-games.md) (Infinite Craft's cache, Death by AI's batch reveal, Hidden Door's ranking, MT-Bench) are referenced, not re-derived.

## 1. Reading the leaderboard — what each column actually measures

Source pages, as fetched 2026-09-01: [Intelligence Benchmarking Methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking) (Intelligence Index **v4.1.1**, no last-updated date on the page; the [v4.1.1 launch article](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v4-1-1) is dated 2026-08-06 and the [v4.1 article](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v4-1) 2026-06-15), [LM API Performance Benchmarking Methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/performance-benchmarking) (**v2.2.0, 2 March 2026**), the [models page FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models) (the leaderboard page itself carries no definitions — it links to this FAQ), the [Intelligence Index evaluation page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index), and the [changelog](https://artificialanalysis.ai/changelog).

### 1a. Column by column

| Column (header text on the leaderboard) | What it is, in AA's words | Role it matters for |
|---|---|---|
| **Intelligence** — "Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index" | "a weighted average of production benchmark scores, scaled from 0 to 100" across nine evals in four categories ([eval page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index); composition in 1b) | Author (coarse prior only — see 1c); Scribe (weak proxy for prose) |
| **Price** — "Cost per Task USD" | Not $/1M tokens. "Weighted average cost (USD) per Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index task … calculated from input, cache hit, cache write, reasoning, and answer token prices, divided by task count, and weighted by its Intelligence Index weight" ([eval page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index); methodology revised 2026-07-30 per [changelog](https://artificialanalysis.ai/changelog)). So a reasoning model's thinking tokens *are* in this number. Per-token pages use blended $/1M at a "3:1 input/output" ratio or a "7:2:1 cache hit/input/output" ratio ([guide](https://artificialanalysis.ai/guide)) | None in v1 (cost is no object); tiebreaker at most |
| **Speed** — "Median Tokens/s" | "Tokens per second received while the model is generating tokens (ie. after first chunk has been received from the API for models which support streaming)" ([models FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models)) | Meter, only if it streams a visible explanation; Scribe if the recap is streamed live |
| **Latency** — "Latency First Chunk (s)" | Defined by the FAQ as **time to first *answer* token**: "Time to first answer token received, in seconds, after API request sent. For reasoning models, this includes the 'thinking' time of the model before providing an answer" ([models FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models)). The methodology separately defines *Time to First Token* (first token, "for reasoning models … the first reasoning token") and *Time to First Answer Token* ("measured after any 'thinking' time") ([performance methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/performance-benchmarking)) — the site's Latency column is the second | **Meter — the column that matters most** |
| **End-to-End Response Time** — "Total Response (s)" | A *computed* figure, not a timed run: "Seconds to receive a 500 token response … calculated based on time to first token, 'thinking' time for reasoning models, and output speed." Thinking time is the "average number of 'reasoning' tokens across a diverse set of 60 prompts … Where the average number of reasoning tokens is not available … we assume 2k reasoning tokens" ([models FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models)) | Meter, as an upper bound on a verbose verdict; otherwise ignore (our outputs are far shorter than 500 tokens) |
| **Context Window** | "Maximum number of combined input & output tokens" ([models FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models)) | Scribe (the Chronicle grows); irrelevant for the Meter |

Filters worth knowing: "Reasoning: All" and "Status: Current". **Reasoning-effort variants are separate rows** (the eval page lists e.g. "(Adaptive Reasoning, Max Effort …)" and "(… Xhigh Effort …)" as distinct entries), so one model name appears at several very different latency and cost points — read the row that matches the effort you would actually configure.

### 1c. How the speed and latency numbers are produced (v2.2.0)

All from the [performance methodology](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/performance-benchmarking) unless noted:

- **Workloads**: "Approximately 1,000 input tokens, at least 1,000 answer tokens"; "Approximately 10,000 input tokens, at least 1,500 answer tokens (default benchmark on our website)"; "Approximately 100,000 input tokens, at least 2,000 answer tokens". The 100-token-input workload is deprecated: "Default speed on our site now reflects results from 10k input token prompts (previously 1k) and we have deprecated performance measurements for 100 token input workloads." Switch workloads with the "Prompt Options" drop-down.
- **Load**: "One prompt is sent to the model's API at a time." A 10-parallel-request test runs "once per day at a random time" on the 1k workload only.
- **Cadence and window**: 1k/10k workloads "8 times per day, approximately every 3 hours"; reported as "the median (P50) over the past 72 hours to reflect sustained changes in performance that users can expect." **Only P50 is published** on the models leaderboard; no p95/p99.
- **Where from**: "Our primary testing server is a virtual machine hosted in Google Cloud's `us-central1-a` zone", and "Time-to-first-token (TTFT) is sensitive to server location as it includes network latency."
- **How**: the "official OpenAI Python library" for OpenAI-compatible endpoints; temperature 0 / 0.6 as above; tokens counted with tiktoken `o200k_base` (not the model's own tokenizer); unique prompt per run because "techniques like speculative decoding mean that output speeds vary with the type of output."
- **Whose endpoint**: "Figures represent performance of the model's first-party API (e.g. OpenAI for o1) or the median across providers where a first-party API is not available" ([models FAQ](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models)). Same-model-different-provider variance is real enough that AA launched a separate [Endpoint Accuracy Index](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/endpoint-accuracy-index): "The same model served by different providers can behave differently depending on quantization, sampling defaults, context handling, token limits, prompt parsers and other endpoint-side configuration."
- **Prompt length matters**: "Longer prompts can result in both longer time to first token and slower output tokens per second compared to shorter prompts." Our Meter prompt is far below 1k tokens, so the leaderboard latency is a pessimistic estimate of *model* latency for us — but the network leg is measured from Iowa, not from our server.

### 1d. What the leaderboard does not capture that our roles depend on

- **Semantic judgment, word association, puzzle construction, misdirection**: none of the nine evals touch it (1b). The Index is agentic work, tool use, coding, long-context QA, factual recall, science exams. AA's own FAQ says "individual benchmark results may be more relevant for specific use cases" ([eval page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/evaluations/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index)) — and for ours, none apply directly.
- **Creative or narrative prose (Scribe)**: no creative-writing eval exists on the site (site search returned none; none is in the "Additional Evaluations" list on the [methodology page](https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking)).
- **Short structured outputs / instruction following (Meter)**: IFBench was removed from the Index in v4.1 — "The benchmark no longer distinguishes frontier models sufficiently" ([v4.1 article](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/artificial-analysis-intelligence-index-v4-1)) — and is reported separately.
- **Run-to-run consistency**: only the aggregate CI is published; per-model variance across repeats is not. Nothing measures whether a model gives the *same* verdict twice (see §3).
- **Tail latency**: P50 over 72h only. A living-room reveal feels its worst call, not its median (see §2).
- **Our prompt shape**: smallest workload is ~1k in / ≥1k out; the leaderboard default is 10k in / ≥1,500 out; E2E assumes a 500-token answer. A Meter verdict is a few hundred tokens in and tens of tokens out.
- **Provider and geography**: first-party endpoint or cross-provider median, measured from us-central1-a.

| Role | When it runs | Columns that matter | Columns to ignore |
|---|---|---|---|

## 2. Latency budgets — what the evidence says people tolerate

Read the leaderboard's Latency column against these numbers. Quotes are from the primary text unless flagged.

### 2a. The classic thresholds, traced to their sources

- **Nielsen's three limits** are a synthesis, not an experiment: "0.1 second is about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously"; "1.0 second is about the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted"; "10 seconds is about the limit for keeping the user's attention focused on the dialogue"; percent-done indicators are for "operations taking more than about 10 seconds" ([NN/g, from *Usability Engineering* ch. 5, 1993](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)). He cites Miller 1968, Card/Robertson/Mackinlay 1991, and Myers 1985.
- **Miller 1968** ([AFIPS](https://doi.org/10.1145/1476589.1476628)) is a reasoned analysis, and the one that speaks to a reveal: "If you address another human being, you expect some communicative response within x seconds—perhaps two to four seconds … silences of more than four seconds become embarrassing because they imply a breaking of the thread of communication." His rule: "response delays of more than two seconds should follow only a condition of task closure as perceived by the human" — and a reveal *is* a closure. Delay tolerance is stepwise, not linear ("psychological step-down discontinuities"). By topic: control activation "No more than 0.1 second"; a complex inquiry "complete response within four seconds"; a status inquiry the user knows requires searching "may be relaxed to seven to ten seconds"; results "within 15 seconds" keep the user "in the problem-solving frame of mind"; "a system with response delays of a standard ten seconds will not permit … thinking continuity."
- **Card, Robertson & Mackinlay 1991** ([CHI '91](https://doi.org/10.1145/108844.108874)) give the 0.1 s / 1 s / 10 s "human time constants" (perceptual processing; immediate response, citing Newell 1990; unit task) and state the 1 s constant as a *turn-taking* rule: "A person can make an unprepared response to some stimulus within about a second. If there is more than a second, then either the listening party makes a backchannel response … (e.g., 'uh-huh') or the speaking party makes a response (e.g., 'uh…') to indicate that he is still thinking" — so their agents "provide status feedback at intervals no longer than this constant." The perceptual-processor cycle behind the 0.1 s is Card, Moran & Newell 1983's "100 [50–200] ms" (parameters as reproduced in [Jastrzembski & Charness 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4591021/); secondary table).
- **Shneiderman 1984** ([ACM Computing Surveys](https://doi.org/10.1145/2514.2517)): "frequent users prefer response times of less than a second for most tasks"; "the frequently mentioned 2-second limit [Miller 1968] seems appropriate for many tasks"; "lengthy response times (over 15 seconds) are generally detrimental." On **variability** — the finding that licenses a fixed-length reveal: "modest variations in response time (plus or minus 50 percent of the mean) appear to be tolerable"; "Frustration may emerge only if delays are unusually long—at least twice the anticipated time"; and "It may be useful to slow down unexpected fast responses to avoid surprising the user."
- Doherty & Thadani 1982 (IBM) argues for sub-second response in repetitive transaction work ([reproduction](https://jlelliotton.blogspot.com/p/the-economic-value-of-rapid-response.html); the "400 ms Doherty threshold" is later folklore) — not our case.

### 2b. Conversational agents: delays, fillers, and the "thinking" frame

- **"Faster is not always better"** ([Gnewuch, Morana, Adam & Maedche, ECIS 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2018_rp/113/)): "dynamic response delays not only increase users' perception of humanness and social presence, but also lead to greater satisfaction" — delay "calculated based on the complexity of the response and complexity of the previous message" (abstract; full text paywalled, so formula, range and N are unverified; the [dataset](https://radar.kit.edu/radar/en/dataset/FdJmclKpjHzLfExE.ExpBot+-+A+dataset+of+79+dialogs+with+an+experimental+customer+service+chatbot) holds 79 dialogs).
- **The replication flips for veterans** ([Gnewuch et al., BISE 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-022-00755-x)): 202 participants; instant ≈ "200 to 400 ms" vs "a delay of 2.3 s on average"; delay raised social presence for novices ("b = 0.69") but was "negative for experienced users (b = −0.51, p < 0.05)"; "no difference in user perceptions between static and dynamic delays"; and the authors could not test the suspected inverted-U beyond 2.3 s. Typing-indicator dots likewise help "only for novice users" ([SIGHCI 2018](https://aisel.aisnet.org/sighci2018/14/)). Explaining *why* the bot is slow adds nothing once the delay is length-scaled ([Zhang et al., CUI '24](https://doi.org/10.1145/3640794.3665550), N=194).
- **Modern LLM-agent numbers.** With embodied agents at 1.5 / 4.0 / 6.5 s: "latency above 4 seconds degrades quality of experience"; 1.5 s was liked most (57.4%), 6.5 s liked least (61.1%); fillers "significantly improved" response-time ratings at 4.0 and 6.5 s ([Maslych et al., CUI '25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22352), N=54). With a chat LLM at 2 / 9 / 20 s to first token (N=240): "responses at 2 s latency (M=5.76) were rated less thoughtful than those at 9 s (M=6.09; p=.008)" and 9 s responses "were rated more useful"; ~31% of free responses inferred "AI Deliberation"; at 20 s some doubted the connection ([Tan et al., 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)). VR agents answer at a median 4.1 s vs a human's 1.2 s ([Cao et al., CHI 2026](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3790947)). Delays of 10–60 s cut young adults' satisfaction 4.11 → 3.29 ([Wang & Lo, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-02459-9), N=160).
- **Human turn-taking, the instinct underneath**: the cross-language mean gap is "+208 ms" ([Stivers et al., PNAS 2009](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903616106)); "after approximately 700 ms, the proportion of dispreferreds was greater" — a long gap predicts bad news ([Kendrick & Torreira, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2014.955997)); 600 and 1200 ms silences read as "less willingness to comply" ([Roberts et al., 2006](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~francisa/Articles/Roberts-etal_SpeechCom06.pdf), N=111); the just-noticeable delay in dialogue is "327.2 ms (±89.7)" and repeated, expected delays produce adaptation ([Kohrs et al., PLoS ONE 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0146250)). A referee is not an interlocutor, but a silent 1–4 s will be read through these reflexes — hence visible activity, never silence.
- **If reasoning streams**: comfortable streaming tops out at "21.20 words per second" for simple and "11.97 words per second" for complex text ([Xiao & Yang, UIST '25](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17999), N=79). No controlled study of streaming-vs-whole perceived latency was found.

### 2c. Games, waits, suspense, and progress indicators

- **Claypool & Claypool 2006** ([CACM](https://doi.org/10.1145/1167838.1167860)): thresholds of 100 ms (first-person), 500 ms (third-person), "1,000 msec" (omnipresent/RTS) by precision and deadline; nothing on turn-based play, which their model places far right of 1 s. A 2024 review calls the waiting-in-games literature "quite scattered" ([Tepponen et al., CHI PLAY '24](https://doi.org/10.1145/3665463.3678791)); **no primary study of acceptable waits at turn-based reveal moments exists** — flagged.
- **Disengagement in the field**: a randomized experiment on 84,325 mobile players found "a grayscale display paired with a 10-second loading delay reduced daily playtime by up to 30.8% and retention rates by 40.4%", with 1 s delays minimal ([Nakamura et al., IMWUT 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712281)). Web delays of 0–12 s: "decreases in performance and behavioral intentions begin to flatten when the delays extend to 4 seconds or longer, and attitudes flatten when the delays extend to 8 seconds" ([Galletta et al., JAIS 2004](https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol5/iss1/1/), N=196); mobile search users "exceeding a certain threshold of 7-10 sec … report feeling significantly more tensed, tired, terrible, frustrated" ([Arapakis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09086)).
- **Suspense is the fun**: "Greater outcome uncertainty led to greater enjoyment, and this effect was mediated by suspense" ([Abuhamdeh, Csikszentmihalyi & Jalal, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9425-2)); formally, "A period has more suspense if the variance of the next period's beliefs is greater" — with game shows among the applications ([Ely, Frankel & Kamenica, JPE 2015](https://doi.org/10.1086/677350)); people "were willing to pay more to experience a kiss delayed by 3 days than an immediate kiss" — savoring is real for fleeting pleasures ([Loewenstein, 1987](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/AnticipationDelayed.pdf), N=30). None of these quantify seconds.
- **Progress and the labor illusion**: in a 5.5 s bar, "participants perceived progress bars with pauses as taking longer … accelerating progress was strongly favored", with both effects exaggerated "towards the end of the process" ([Harrison et al., UIST '07](https://doi.org/10.1145/1294211.1294231), N=22); the best visual trick saves ~11% of perceived duration ([Harrison, Yeo & Hudson, CHI 2010](https://doi.org/10.1145/1753326.1753556)); with operational transparency, people chose a waiting service over an instant one "when waiting for both 30 seconds (62%) and 60 seconds (63%)" versus "42% … and just 23%" without — but the benefit "began to decline after 30 seconds", and with unfavorable outcomes "people blame the service for this failure" — transparent waiting scored worst (M=2.47 vs 3.24 instant) ([Buell & Norton, Management Science 2011](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376)).

### 2d. What it adds up to (evidence-level, before our inference in §5)

- **Instant (≤ 0.1–0.3 s)** applies to the tap, not the grade: Miller's 0.1 s, the 100 ms perceptual cycle, Kohrs's ~330 ms just-noticeable delay.
- **"The machine is thinking" (≈ 1–4 s comfortable; to ~8 s with visible work)**: past 1 s a backchannel is mandatory (Card 1991); Miller allows > 2 s at closure, 4 s for a complex answer, 7–10 s when work is expected; ~4 s is the modern silence ceiling (Maslych), fillers recover 4–6.5 s; a 9 s framed wait read as *more* thoughtful (Tan); attitudes bottom out by ~8 s (Galletta) and frustration starts at 7–10 s (Arapakis).
- **Disengagement (~10 s; hard wall 15 s)**: Nielsen's 10 s, Miller's 15 s, Shneiderman's ">15 seconds", Nakamura's 10 s field losses, the labor illusion decaying after 15–30 s.
- **What choreography can honestly mask**: a fixed 2–4 s beat is supported by Shneiderman's ±50%-variability rule and "slow down unexpected fast responses", Miller's closure exception, and the suspense literature; inside it, accelerate toward the reveal and never pause near the end (Harrison). It cannot stretch past ~4–5 s without filler-style activity, past ~8–10 s without an explicit status signal, and a long effortful wait makes a harsh grade land harder (Buell & Norton exp. 5).

## 3. Model tier vs. quality for semantic judging and puzzle construction

Models are described by tier/size and vintage, not name. "Frontier" = the strongest commercial models of the paper's date; "reasoning-tier" = models that think before answering.

### 3a. Constructing and playing word puzzles (the Author's job)

- **Rule-following is the first filter, and small models fail it.** In the Codenames benchmark ([Stephenson, Sidji & Ronval, arXiv 2412.11373](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11373)), 14 models were tried and 9 kept: "many of the smaller models were not able to correctly adhere to the game's specified rules and output format, resulting in repeated invalid clues or guesses that frequently devolved into pure random play" (a 7B, a ~14B, an 8×7B MoE, a 3B-class, and a small commercial model were dropped). Among survivors, self-paired mean turns to clear a board ran from 8.41 (largest reasoning-tier) through ~10.4–11.0 (2024 frontier chat models) to 11.1–11.2 (small reasoning-tier variants). The [GemBench Codenames study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.gem-1.63/) ([Hakimov, Pfennigschmidt & Schlangen, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11707)) is blunter about the floor: an 8B open model completed only 52.3% of its games (clemscore 14.6) versus 49.2 for the best small reasoning-tier commercial model, "the only model that played all episodes without once making an instruction following error"; "as expected, larger models perform better" — though non-monotonically (a 405B scored below its 70B sibling), and "All models achieved a perfect score for the easy set" while on difficult associations "no model reaches 30 points."
- **Risk appetite is a model trait, not a setting.** Average clue number ranged 1.30 to 3.40 across models and correlated with loss rate at Spearman 0.821 ([Stephenson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11373)) — cap it in the prompt.
- **Model-vs-model play does not measure human guessability.** Static embeddings paired with themselves beat every LLM (GloVe–GloVe 5.24 turns, 0% assassin losses) but "collapse" when mismatched ([Stephenson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11373)); the JAIR study behind design law 7 makes the point with its "duke"-for-"slug" clue: "when one word embedding method judges another, it is possible for the clue-giver embedding method to obtain 'high performance' in spite of producing nonsensical clues" ([Koyyalagunta et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.05885)). Consequence: the Meter cannot be validated by agreeing with the Author; only human-labelled pairs count.
- **Lateral (Connections-style) categories lag human experts at every tier.** Best 2024 frontier full-solve rate 18% of 438 puzzles versus experts solving "over 60%" perfectly ([Samadarshi et al., EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11012)); frontier 29.2% → 38.9% with chain-of-thought, an earlier mid-tier model 6.4% ([Todd et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.11730)); with hints, frontier ~37–40% vs 70B ~24–29% vs humans 56–61%, and three frontier models "show no statistically significant differences from each other" ([Naeini et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.01621)). Hardest categories are wordplay — "non-semantic properties of words."
- **Frontier-tier generation of puzzles is already competitive with editors on some axes.** A frontier model (late-2023) with a tree-of-thoughts pipeline produced Connections puzzles that 52 users solved at 58.5% vs 69.1% for real NYT puzzles; its "false connection" puzzles were judged equal-or-better than NYT on creativity 42.9% of the time (tied 14.3%) and its overlap puzzles harder 60% of the time ([Merino et al., AIIDE 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.11240)). Recurring defects: category names that misrepresent the link, "the same limited set of categories", and over-generic categories; open-weight models "are not as capable at following the complicated system prompts" (preliminary, no numbers). Crossword-clue generation reaches ~72% top-rated / 81% acceptable ([Clue-Instruct, arXiv 2404.06186](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.06186); snippet-only, unverified).
- Social note: in a 54-participant Codenames study the LLM clue assistant "enhanced players' convergent and divergent thinking, but interfered with formation of team mental models" and "challenged many players' understanding of the 'spirit of the game'" ([Sidji, Smith & Rogerson, CHI PLAY 2024](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3677081); abstract only) — a reminder that ADR-0001's "no player seat" is also a trust decision.

### 3b. How human-like are LLM meaning-closeness judgments? (the Meter's job)

- **Frontier-tier similarity ratings sit at or above human inter-annotator agreement.** A 2023 frontier model at temperature 0 correlated with humans at Spearman 0.86 on SimLex-999, 0.81 on SimVerb-3500, and 0.82 on contextual relatedness (human inter-annotator 0.79); "in all but one dataset … GPT-4's correlation with human ratings was at least as high as average inter-annotator agreement" ([Trott, Behavior Research Methods 2024](https://d-nb.info/1325131660/34)). Systematic errors worth designing around: antonyms rated more similar than humans rate them (mean difference 2.53 on the scale), larger errors on concrete pairs, and relatedness over-estimated across senses.
- **A 70B open model matches on similarity but is measurably less human-like on association.** Against 12,281 human free-association cues, a 2024 frontier model's derived relatedness was statistically indistinguishable from human-derived networks on three similarity datasets; the 70B model matched on SimLex-999 only and was significantly lower on the other two; top-100 associate overlap with humans 54% vs 43% ([Xiao et al., CoNLL 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.conll-1.38/)).
- **7B-class and small commercial models collapse in diversity, not in priming.** Against the same human norms: unique responses 116,640 (humans) vs 105,367 (8B), 41,369 (7B), 15,275 (small commercial); edge overlap with the human network 23% / 13% / 15% — yet simulated priming effects were human-like at every size ([Abramski et al., Scientific Data 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.01330)). Small models know what is related; they know far fewer ways.
- For prose (the Scribe): on human-likeness of generated text, "scaling up model size does not enhance human-likeness" ([Pagan et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04195)) — tier is the weakest lever there.

### 3c. LLM-as-judge reliability by tier

- **Baseline (2023):** the strongest judge agreed with human experts 85% on MT-Bench (human–human 81%) and 87% on Chatbot Arena; position-swap consistency 65.0% for the strongest judge (77.5% with few-shot) vs 46.2% and 23.8% for two mid-tier judges; a verbosity attack fooled the strongest judge 8.7% of the time vs 91.3% for the others; reference-guided grading cut math-grading failures "from 70% to 15%"; and in disagreements, humans found the judge's reasoning reasonable 75% of the time and changed their vote 34% of the time ([Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) — the source of design law 4's biases). Position bias concentrates on close calls: order-swap conflict 46.3% on close pairs vs 5.0% on distant ones for the strongest judge, 82.5% vs 52.5% for a mid-tier one ([Wang et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)).
- **Alignment rises with size, then plateaus well below human–human.** Cohen's κ with humans on a high-agreement task (×100; values read from a figure, approximate): strongest commercial 84, 70B open 79–80, 7B 66–72, 2B 50, human–human 96: "only the best (and largest) models achieve reasonable alignment with humans. However, they are still quite far behind inter-human agreement" ([Thakur et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)). Human agreement by judge size in a controlled sweep: 0.36 (0.5B), 0.45 (7B), 0.48 (32B), 0.50 (72B) ([arXiv 2501.17178](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17178v1)). A 2025 sweep of 54 judges puts the floor lower than expected: a 12B judge reached κ 0.774 against human–human 0.801, no 1–3B model qualified, and "judge excellence is not solely dependent on model size but on specific training strategies" ([Judge's Verdict, arXiv 2510.09738](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.09738)).
- **Hard items separate tiers; easy items don't.** On JudgeBench's objectively-checkable pairs, a 2024 frontier chat model scored "no better than random guessing" with a vanilla prompt (50.9%; 56.6% with a better one), reasoning-tier judges 75.4–80.9%, a fine-tuned 7B judge 34.9% ([Tan et al., ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12784)).
- **Position bias is the tier-linked failure; verbosity bias has faded.** Across 21 judges from 9 providers (~541k judgments, 2026): position bias 0.002 (frontier) to 0.192 (8B open) — "Reasoning and frontier models have systematically lower rates of position bias; small and cost-optimized models have the highest rates"; verbosity bias "below 0.011" for all; and "A judge reporting 85% agreement on MT-Bench has κ≈0.48" — report κ, not agreement ([Reliability without Validity, arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)).
- **Pairwise beats absolute scoring, and helps weak judges most.** Direct scoring → pairwise Spearman with humans: 7B 0.02 → 0.43, mid-tier 0.44 → 0.56, frontier 0.55 → 0.64 ([Liu et al., COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.16950)); surveys concur that "pairwise comparative assessments outperform other judging methods in terms of positional consistency" ([Gu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594)) and that pointwise↔pairwise conversions "are not always reliable" ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.05579)). Panels of three small judges beat one large judge on QA correctness (κ 0.763 vs 0.627 on NQ) at 7–8× lower cost and with less self-preference ([Verga et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)).
- **Prompt knobs, measured** ([arXiv 2501.17178](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17178v1)): "The pair format performs better"; "Increasing temperature negatively impacts performance"; averaging both orderings "gives a performance boost"; few-shot helps large judges and confuses small ones; JSON "does not impact much the performance"; and — the one tension with design law 3 — "Asking the judge to provide an explanation, or its answer hurts performance" in that study, whereas the surveys report explain-then-rate correlates better with humans ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.05579)). Shown reasoning is a player-facing feature; validate its effect on accuracy separately.

### 3d. Consistency across repeated calls — no tier gives you determinism

- **Vendors say so.** OpenAI's `seed`: "our system will make a best effort to sample deterministically … Determinism is not guaranteed" ([SDK source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openai/openai-python/main/src/openai/types/chat/completion_create_params.py); [cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/reproducible_outputs_with_the_seed_parameter)). Anthropic: "even with `temperature` of `0.0`, the results will not be fully deterministic" ([Messages API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/messages)). Google's docs are internally inconsistent — the prompting guide calls temperature 0 "deterministic" ([prompting strategies](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/prompting-strategies)) while the SDK reference says a fixed seed is "best effort" ([reference](https://googleapis.github.io/js-genai/release_docs/interfaces/types.GenerateContentConfig.html)).
- **Measured.** Temperature 0, fixed seed, 10 runs: "accuracy variations up to 15% across naturally occurring runs", and "none of the LLMs consistently delivers repeatable accuracy across all tasks, much less identical output strings"; identical-string rates hit 0% on several tasks for frontier and 70B models, with no size pattern ([Atil et al., arXiv 2408.04667](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667)). 1,000 temperature-0 completions of one prompt on a 235B open model yielded 80 unique completions, identical through token 102 ([He et al., Thinking Machines, 2025-09-10](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). Root cause: "the primary reason nearly all LLM inference endpoints are nondeterministic is that the load (and thus batch-size) nondeterministically varies" — server-side, outside any caller's control; their batch-invariant kernels made all 1,000 identical at roughly 1.6–2× the time.
- **Judge test–retest.** Krippendorff's α across three identical runs: 0.33 (70B), 0.63 (distilled reasoning), 0.79 (32B); majority vote of three runs beat the best single run ([Rating Roulette, arXiv 2510.27106](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27106)); "strong proprietary models are not necessarily consistent evaluators" ([arXiv 2412.00543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00543)); across 21 judges, test–retest ran 0.889–0.992 (mean 0.944) and "the most reproducible judges are among the least valid" ([arXiv 2606.19544](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)). Run-to-run noise does not track tier; bias does.
- **The fix is architectural, not a model choice**: cache every verdict by key (design law 1, Infinite Craft), judge both orderings and accept only agreement, majority-vote borderline calls. Determinism is a property of *our* store, never of the endpoint.

### 3e. Short structured verdicts

- Forcing JSON hurt reasoning tasks in 2024 — a mid-tier model fell 76.0% → 49.3% on GSM8K, a small commercial model 86.5% → 23.4% — because "100% of GPT 3.5 Turbo JSON-mode responses placed the 'answer' key before the 'reason' key, resulting in zero-shot direct answering"; classification tasks were unaffected and a two-step natural-language-then-format flow gave "nearly identical performance" ([Tam et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442)). A rebuttal with true constrained decoding and matched prompts found structured ≥ unstructured on an 8B model ([dottxt](https://blog.dottxt.ai/say-what-you-mean.html); vendor blog), as did grammar-constrained decoding benchmarks (GSM8K 80.1 → 83.8 on an 8B; [JSONSchemaBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10868)).
- The 2026 reconciliation: the format penalty "depends strongly on a model's spare capacity" — negligible for a frontier model (88.7% JSON vs 89.3% free on MATH-Hard), −28 to −36 points for mini/small tiers; recommendation "think first, format later" ([Capacity, Not Format, arXiv 2606.09410](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09410)). For a Meter verdict: reasoning field before verdict field (or free-text reasoning then a structured tail), and the bigger the model the safer a bare enum.

## 4. Latency-hiding patterns, with sources

Each pattern: what it buys (network / TTFT / generation / *perceived*), where it is documented, and its fit for a reveal where two committed strings are graded.

### 4a. Compute before the reveal, not at it (buys: everything, for anything computable ahead)

- **Cache-once, deterministic forever.** Infinite Craft's pair cache (see [llm-in-word-games.md](llm-in-word-games.md); the architecture is third-party-observed — [O'Dwyer's reverse-engineering](https://quuxplusone.github.io/blog/2024/02/08/infinite-craft/): "Only completely new pairings are sent to the LLM, and then the response cached for posterity" — not a first-party statement). Design law 1 already mandates this for verdicts.
- **Generate offline, rank online.** Hilary Mason (Hidden Door, [interview 2025-10-20](https://aigamechangers.substack.com/p/the-machine-is-not-itself-creative)): "You can do generation offline, when you have the luxury for quality control, and ranking in real time." When a Page's vocabulary is finite, every candidate pair can be pre-graded by the Author, making the live Meter a table lookup with the LLM as the cache-miss path.
- **Fire at commit, not at reveal.** Both players commit before anyone taps "reveal"; the verdict call can start at the second commit. OpenAI's latency guide endorses going further: "If the steps *are* strictly sequential, however, you might still be able to leverage speculative execution. This is particularly effective for classification steps where one outcome is more likely than the others" ([latency optimization guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)). Speculative next-action research reports "up to 55% next-action prediction accuracy, translating into up to 20% latency reductions" ([Ye et al., arXiv 2510.04371](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04371)) — modest in general, but our answer space at commit time is tiny.
- **Prerender the reveal screen.** Web analogue: Speculation Rules — "Future navigations to a prerendered page will be near-instant" ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Speculation_Rules_API)); Chrome prerenders on a 200 ms hover in `moderate` eagerness ([Chrome docs](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/prerender-pages)). The laptop can have the reveal frame paint-ready on the "both committed" event.

### 4b. Kill the tail (buys: p99, which is what one live moment feels)

- **Hedged requests.** Dean & Barroso, ["The Tail at Scale," CACM 2013](https://cacm.acm.org/research/the-tail-at-scale/): "issue the same request to multiple replicas and use the results from whichever replica responds first … The client cancels remaining outstanding requests once the first result is received." Their canonical number: "sending a hedging request after a 10ms delay reduces the 99.9th-percentile latency … from 1,800ms to 74ms while sending just 2% more requests." They defer the hedge until "the 95th-percentile expected latency" to cap extra load at ~5% — a constraint we don't have.
- **LLM-specific hedging note** (practitioner, secondary): calibrate the hedge timer on true time-to-first-token, not time-to-first-byte; TTFT-calibrated hedging kept p99 gains at "17–19.8% overhead" where TTFB-calibrated cost ~100% ([InfoQ, 2026-05-28](https://www.infoq.com/articles/adaptive-hedged-requests-p99-latency/)).
- **Vendor fast tiers** compress variance rather than the median: OpenAI's Fast mode claims "up to 2.5× faster speeds and more consistent latency" for a per-token premium ([docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/fast-mode)); Anthropic's Priority Tier "prioritizes requests in this tier over all other requests" but the page now says capacity commitments "are no longer available for purchase" ([service tiers](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/service-tiers)).

### 4c. Shrink the critical-path call (buys: generation time, then TTFT)

- OpenAI's seven principles, verbatim: "1. Process tokens faster. 2. Generate fewer tokens. 3. Use fewer input tokens. 4. Make fewer requests. 5. Parallelize. 6. Make your users wait less. 7. Don't default to an LLM." With the sizing rule that matters: "Cutting 50% of your output tokens may cut ~50% of your latency," whereas "cutting 50% of your prompt may only result in a 1–5% latency improvement" ([latency optimization guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)).
- Anthropic's [reduce-latency doc](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-latency): "The fewer tokens the model has to process and generate, the faster the response will be"; use `max_tokens` as "a hard limit", noting it "is usually most appropriate for multiple choice or short answer responses where the answer comes right at the beginning" — exactly a verdict's shape; and "It's always better to first engineer a prompt that works well without model or prompt constraints, and then try latency reduction strategies afterward."
- **Field order is generation order** in structured output ("Structured outputs follow the same order as the provided schema" — [Azure OpenAI docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/openai/how-to/structured-outputs)), so a `verdict`-first schema streams the grade earliest — but a practitioner test found reasoning-then-answer scored 46.67% vs answer-then-reasoning 33.33% on his set ([Castillo, 2024-11-09](https://dylancastillo.co/posts/llm-pydantic-order-matters.html); secondary, small n). The documented escape is **two stages**: a tiny enum-only verdict call, then a separate explanation call streamed to the laptop. Field evidence that prompt trimming helps TTFT: Ubisoft's AI-teammates team cut a 10k+ token prompt by 30% to lower TTFT, targeting a mic-to-reaction loop "in under two seconds" and landing at 1.5 s ([Computer Weekly, 2026-05-12](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366642940/In-video-games-of-the-future-your-AI-teammates-will-actually-listen); trade press quoting the engineers).

### 4d. Choreograph the wait (buys: perceived)

- **Duplex's disfluencies** ([Google Research blog, 2018](https://research.google/blog/google-duplex-an-ai-system-for-accomplishing-real-world-tasks-over-the-phone/)): "hmm"s and "uh"s are "added when combining widely differing sound units … or adding synthetic waits, which allows the system to signal in a natural way that it is still processing"; "it's important for latency to match people's expectations … after people say something simple, e.g., 'hello?', they expect an instant response"; and "in some situations, we found it was actually helpful to introduce more latency to make the conversation feel more natural."
- **Filler research**: contextual fillers "positively affected participants' rating of the agent's response time but did not impact the agent's likeability" (n=360; [Boukaram et al., HAI 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3472307.3484181)); for voice agents, "latency above 4 seconds degrades quality of experience, while natural conversational fillers improve perceived response time, especially in high-delay conditions" ([Maslych et al., CUI 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.22352)); an incremental system that says "let's see" "has a shorter response time and is perceived as more efficient by the users" ([Skantze & Hjalmarsson, SIGDIAL 2010](https://aclanthology.org/W10-4301/)).
- **Party-game pacing**: Jackbox — "make them aware that the program is waiting for them … We're kind of doing timing and pacing that they might expect from a TV show" ([Built In Chicago](https://www.builtinchicago.org/articles/jackbox-games-design-party-pack); trade interview).
- **Perceived-duration tricks**: progress bars "with animated ribbing that move backwards in a decelerating manner … reduces the perceived duration among our participants by 11%" ([Harrison, Yeo & Hudson, CHI 2010](https://www.chrisharrison.net/projects/progressbars2/ProgressBarsHarrison.pdf)); skeleton screens put "the focus … on content being loaded not the fact that its loading" ([LukeW, 2013](https://www.lukew.com/ff/entry.asp?1797)); the labor illusion — "people can actually prefer websites with longer waits to those that return instantaneous results—even when those results are identical" when the site signals "that they are exerting effort" ([Buell & Norton, 2011](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Norton_Michael_The%20labor%20illusion%20How%20operational_f4269b70-3732-4fc4-8113-72d0c47533e0.pdf)).
- **Games stretch animation over round-trips**: "RTS games typically hide this by providing audio feedback immediately and/or playing cosmetic animation" ([Gaffer on Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/what_every_programmer_needs_to_know_about_game_networking/)); Sony's patent [US 9,674,267](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9674267B2/en) "hiding latency in network multiplayer games" lengthens an animation's run-time while awaiting the peer's reply. Namco's loading-screen-minigame patent [US 5,718,632](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5718632A/en) expired 2015-11-27. No turn-based card-game postmortem stating "animation covers the server round-trip" was found — only the above.

### 4e. Streaming and caching (buys: perceived for long outputs; TTFT for long prefixes — both small for the Meter)

- Streaming is "The single most effective approach, as it cuts the *waiting* time to a second or less … There's a huge difference between **waiting** and **watching progress happen**" ([OpenAI latency guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)); it "can significantly improve the perceived responsiveness" ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-latency)). It does nothing for a one-token verdict; it is the Scribe's tool.
- Prompt caching's latency win is real only for long prefixes: Anthropic's own table shows 10k-token prompts going 1.6 s → 1.1 s (−31%) versus 100k-token prompts 11.5 s → 2.4 s ([announcement](https://claude.com/blog/prompt-caching)); minimum cacheable lengths are 512–4,096 tokens depending on model, with `max_tokens: 0` "cache pre-warming" documented to "eliminate the cache-miss latency penalty on the first user interaction" ([prompt caching docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). OpenAI's minimum is 1,024–2,048 tokens ([docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching)). A Meter prompt below the minimum gets nothing unless padded with a rubric block.

## 5. Signals for our design

Everything below is **inference** from §§1–4. Facts are linked above; the connecting judgment is ours. Cost is no object in v1, so the Price column is ignored throughout and duplicate/hedged calls are treated as free.

### 5a. How to weigh the four columns, per role

| Role | Intelligence | Latency (first *answer* token) | Throughput (tokens/s) | Price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Author** | Dominant — but the Index is an entrance filter, not a measure of our task; our own eval decides | Irrelevant (runs unattended before play) | Irrelevant | Ignored |
| **Meter** | A threshold, not a maximum: strong enough to pass our judging eval; reasoning/frontier tier preferred for low position bias | **Dominant**, read at the effort row you'd actually run | Minor: ~60-token verdicts; matters only for a streamed explanation | Ignored |
| **Scribe** | Secondary — no prose eval exists; the Index barely predicts taste | Irrelevant if generated off the clock | Only if a recap streams live: reading speed suffices | Ignored |

### 5b. Author — "maximize intelligence, ignore the clock"

- Run the highest-effort reasoning-tier row available, unattended, the night before (design law 4; Hidden Door's "generation offline"). Nothing about speed is in the loop.
- Treat the Intelligence column as a shortlist only. What separates models on *our* task is (i) rule- and format-following — small models were dropped from the Codenames benchmark for "repeated invalid clues," open-weight models could not follow Merino's construction prompts (§3a); (ii) lateral category quality, where even frontier models solve 18–40% of Connections puzzles and all tiers fail wordplay (§3a); (iii) risk appetite, a per-model trait (Spearman 0.821 with losses) — cap it in the prompt.
- Generate many candidates, then rank with a separate judge pass. Never validate the Author by the Meter agreeing with it — model-vs-model agreement rewards nonsense clues (Koyyalagunta's "duke" for "slug"). Only human-labelled pairs count.
- The Author's most valuable output is invisible: **pre-grade every candidate pair in the Page's vocabulary**. That turns the live Meter into a lookup (§4a), makes verdicts deterministic by construction (design law 1), and leaves the LLM as the cache-miss path for open-vocabulary answers (Mind-Meld's free words).

### 5c. Meter — "first answer token fast, verdict short and structured, unbiased enough, deterministic by cache"

- **Pick by the Latency column at the exact effort level you'd run.** For reasoning rows it already includes thinking time (§1a). Our prompt is far below the 10k-token workload, so the model leg will be faster than listed; the network leg is measured from Iowa, not our VPS; and only P50 is published — measure your own p95 (§5f).
- **Qualifying rule of thumb**: `Latency + 60 ÷ Speed ≤ 2.5 s` at the leaderboard's P50 lands the full verdict inside the reveal beat (§5d); allow ~1.5× margin for the living room. A row with Latency ≤ ~1 s is comfortable headroom; ≤ 2 s is the ceiling.
- **Tier**: default to a frontier-tier model at low or no reasoning effort. Frontier and reasoning tiers carry near-zero position bias (0.002 vs 0.192 for an 8B) and hold up on hard pairs (§3c); a 12B–70B-class judge is acceptable only if it passes our κ test, and pairwise format lifts weaker judges most. Verbosity bias is no longer a tier issue (§3c).
- **Call shape**: brief reasoning *then* verdict (answer-first JSON is where small and mid tiers lose 28–36 points; §3e); a closeness grade plus one-sentence reason in ≤ ~60 tokens; `max_tokens` clamped (§4c). Judge **both orderings in parallel**, accept agreement, majority-of-3 on borderline pairs (Wang's 46% conflict rate on close calls; Rating Roulette's majority-vote gain). Write the winning verdict, model id, and prompt version to the Chronicle so precedent stays attributable when a model is retired.
- **Hedge identical requests, never different models.** Racing two models would let the ruling depend on which endpoint won — a consistency leak. Send the same request twice (two endpoints or two sends), take the first, cancel the other (§4b). At the hard deadline, fall back to the pre-graded table or a deterministic embedding score, shown as *provisional* and replaced in the Chronicle when the LLM verdict lands.
- **Throughput arithmetic**: a 60-token verdict is 1.2 s of generation at 50 tok/s and 0.4 s at 150 tok/s — real, but second to first-answer-token. An explanation streamed to the laptop needs only reading speed: ~12 words/s for dense text ≈ 16–20 tok/s (§2b).

### 5d. The reveal budget (concrete)

Clock starts when the second player commits (t = 0), which is before anyone taps "reveal."

| Moment | Budget | Why |
|---|---|---|
| t = 0 | Fire both-ordering verdict calls, hedged; prerender the reveal screen on the laptop | §4a, §4b |
| Tap "reveal" | Acknowledge ≤ 100 ms; start a **fixed 2.5 s reveal beat** — needle/drumroll, accelerating, no pause near the end | Miller 0.1 s; Shneiderman's ±50% variability rule and "slow down unexpected fast responses"; Miller's > 2 s-at-closure exception; Harrison's end effect (§2) |
| p50 | First answer token ≤ 1.5 s, full verdict ≤ 2.5 s — inside the beat, so the felt wait beyond the beat is zero | §2d "thinking" tier; §5c rule of thumb |
| p95 | ≤ 4 s: if the verdict is late, extend the beat with visible activity (needle still moving, a "hmm"-style filler), never a static screen | Maslych's 4 s silence ceiling and filler rescue; Card's 1 s backchannel rule; Duplex (§2b, §4d) |
| Hard deadline | 8 s → provisional fallback verdict; the reveal never passes 10 s | Galletta's attitudes flatten at 8 s; Arapakis 7–10 s; Nielsen 10 s; Nakamura's 10 s field losses (§2c) |
| Harsh grades | Shorten the beat; let the shown reasoning do the softening afterward | Buell & Norton exp. 5: effortful waits make bad outcomes land harder (§2c) |
| Shown reasoning | Stream at ≤ 12 words/s; the couple's overrule control appears with it, not after | Xiao & Yang (§2b); design law 3 |

Two cautions the literature adds: the "thinking delay feels good" effect belongs to chatbot novices and to framed deliberation (Gnewuch 2022; Tan 2026) — my wife is a daily word-game player, so keep the beat short and dramatic rather than long and "human"; and a 9 s wait reading as *thoughtful* in a chat study does not transfer to a couch where two people are staring at a laptop — the game-field data (Nakamura, Galletta) is the binding constraint.

### 5e. Scribe — "taste over tier; run off the clock"

- Generate the Chronicle entry right after the Page (while the couple is still talking) or nightly; generate the "previously on…" recap *before* the session opens, since it depends only on the Chronicle. No live wait exists, so Latency and Speed columns are moot.
- The Index has no prose eval, and scaling "does not enhance human-likeness" of text (§3b); choose by reading five sample recaps with my wife, then pin the model per Chapter (design law 1; Suck Up's silent-swap lesson in [llm-in-word-games.md](llm-in-word-games.md)).
- Context Window is the one leaderboard column that grows in importance as the Chronicle does.

### 5f. Three tiny evals we own (the leaderboard shortlists; these decide)

1. **Judging eval**: ~100 word/phrase pairs, each graded 0–100 by Jacob and his wife independently and then reconciled. Score candidate Meters by Spearman and κ against the reconciled labels, in both orderings, three repeats each to measure test–retest. Report κ, not agreement (85% agreement ≈ κ 0.48; §3c).
2. **Author eval**: ten generated Pages; code checks rule compliance; the couple rates fairness of misdirection and "would we play this."
3. **Latency eval**: 100 calls of the exact Meter prompt from the VPS at evening hours; take p50 and p95 of first-answer-token and total time; apply §5c's rule of thumb to the measured numbers rather than the leaderboard's.
